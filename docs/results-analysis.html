What the metric supports

Redundancy can cost credit

Under the official α=0.2, β=0.8, 300 m distance-weighted Tversky metric, an unsupported positive adds false-positive mass. If a dense detector has already saturated nearby truth coverage, thinning redundant pixels can reduce the penalty while preserving true-positive credit. That makes the owner's sparse-thinning explanation plausible.

What remains unknown

Which TIFF earned which score?

The official public board shows participant names and score values, not filenames. Its rank-13 row at 0.2778 has the same number as the user-supplied H33 score association, but it does not identify the H33 artifact. Numerical coincidence is not attribution.

Reported 0.2778 claim: reconcile, don't repeat

The user-provided ledger associated an H33-2-B2 filename with 0.2778. The public GEMSDOE32 owner site, when reviewed, instead described its 37,654-pixel file as a candidate with a model-projected 0.2747 and stated that no artifact had an organizer score at the time. The official leaderboard separately listed participant extradr19 at 0.2778, but does not expose the submitted file. These records disagree in evidence class, so this repository records the H33 association as user-reported, not independently verified.

This is an example of why “high-scoring prior submission” analysis should separate the official leaderboard from a team's methods report. The public top three cannot be scientifically explained from scores alone. No claim is made about their private models, data, feature engineering or submissions.

Why 0.3262 is a difficult target

The visible gap from a correctly attributed 0.2778 artifact to the dated public leader at 0.3262 would be 0.0484 DTI, but the 0.2778 attribution is unresolved. Even if it were correct, that arithmetic does not establish a path to closing the gap. First obtain verified inputs, reproduce the metric against the official worked example, and evaluate a novel method at matched budget under spatial separation.
